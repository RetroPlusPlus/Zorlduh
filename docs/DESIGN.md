# Zorlduh — design context

Authored at inception, 2026-11-10. What the project is, what is decided, and what stays true however the
implementation changes.

## What it is

Two things, in this order of priority for now:

1. **The start of an ecosystem library for Polyrhythm.** The engine is deliberately low-level: a set of
   primitives that compose orthogonally. This project grows the layer above them — reusable,
   distributable components that compose the primitives into higher-level features and lower the skill
   floor for making a game on the engine, the way the libraries around React make React easier to use.
   It starts from the components that the engine's existing component-style consumers each wrote for
   themselves. Packaging the components as separate modules comes later; every system written now is
   written so it can be lifted out whole.
2. **A top-down action-adventure game**, in the tradition of the genre's classic 2D entries: a world
   explored on foot, a sword, hearts, enemies, items, dungeons. The game is the proving ground that
   decides what the library needs. For now it is a **technical template** — the systems any game of this
   kind needs — with no game-specific design yet. The game's own design opens when the template is done.

**Name:** Zorlduh. Executable, CMake prefix and C++ namespace `zorlduh`. Engine identity: organization
`Retro++`, application `Zorlduh`.

## Visual direction

- **Game Boy Color idiom:** viewport 160×144, 8×8 tile cells, 4-color palettes, 16×16 characters built
  from four cells, window scale 4.
- **Timing: a clean 60 Hz tick**, not the Game Boy's 59.73 Hz — `TickPeriodNs::Hz60` with no CPU model.
  Every duration in the game is counted in ticks, so a tick is 1/60 s.
- **Placeholder art** until real art replaces it: generated by a script, deterministic, committed. It
  makes behavior visible; it does not set a look.
- **Color is indexed (CLUT).** A sheet holds palette indices and no color; color comes from a
  palette image chosen per tile or sprite when it is drawn. Sheets are grayscale at the depth their
  indices need (2-bit for four colors), so each one reads by eye as its art. Maps are grayscale too,
  their ids spread across the range so a map reads by eye.
- **Assets are laid out by kind, then category:** `assets/gfx/`, `assets/palettes/`, `assets/maps/`
  (later `assets/audio/`), each with folders such as `tiles/`, `sprites/` and `hud/`; under `maps/`, one
  folder per layout and in it one per area, holding the area's tile map, its collision map and a map
  for each further layer it is drawn in. Every asset is baked into the executable.

## Tone and writing voice

None yet. The template has no text, story or characters. Decided when the game's own design opens.

## Mechanical identity

The template's verbs and systems:

- **Screen:** a 16 px HUD strip across the top; a 160×128 play area (20×16 cells) beneath it.
- **The world is layouts of areas joined by openings.** An area — an overworld screen, a dungeon room, a
  cave — is its own map, of any size in cells, drawn through its own art; nothing outside an area
  exists, so nothing outside one is ever drawn, and beyond an area's edge is the void. A layout is a set
  of areas that share a cell size, a view and their transitions' settings — the overworld is one, each
  dungeon another, and a forest of rooms joined only by seams a third. Areas have no position in a larger world: an opening in one leads to a named opening
  in another, in its own layout or any other, and that is the only link between them, so a world of any
  shape can be built — an overworld, every floor of a dungeon, the houses in a town. An opening is a gap
  in a wall (any number per wall) or a spot inside the area (stairs, a cave mouth), and each says how it
  is gone through: a **scroll**, where the next area slides in lined up with the gap and the player is
  carried through; a **fade** to the entrance; or a **seam**, where the next area is simply there beyond
  the wall, drawn whenever it is in view, and the player walks on into it with nothing on screen moving.
  What is drawn reaches through chains of seams — a room hanging off the side of the next room is in
  view from this one — as many seams out as the layout allows (any number by default); the camera's
  reach and what is solid go one seam, to the next area only.
  A scroll or a seam may shift the next area from where the gaps line it up, by whole cells. Links are
  one-way, so a two-way door is two openings leading to each other and a one-way drop is one; a seam
  leads back by construction. The world's model is the WorldKit module; the camera and its laws are
  CameraKit's.
- **An area is drawn in layers.** Its collision map says what is solid; its look is any number of tile
  maps of its size, each drawn through its own art on a layer of its own at its own depth — the ground,
  a path under the player's feet, a canopy or a roof over the player's head. Among them are the area's
  **actor slots**: named depths where the things that move in the area are drawn, over the layers below
  and under the layers above. Every depth is the game's, from one depth table, and the world is handed
  them; the player and everything else that walks the world are drawn at the `"actors"` slot, and an
  area may have more slots (things that fly over a canopy, things that swim under a surface). A layer
  that covers part of an area draws nothing elsewhere, through its sheet's transparent index; whatever
  is drawn over the whole world — effects, the HUD — takes a depth above the world's highest.
- **The camera moves only with the player.** Within an area it centers on the player and stops at the
  area's edges, reaching across a seam into the area beyond. It moves only when the player moves,
  toward where it should be and never past it, never against the player, never on its own. A scroll
  slides it along the door's axis only; a seam is crossed with nothing moving on screen. Where a
  transition leaves the camera away from an area's own place, with void beside the area, the camera
  stays there and comes into place only as the player walks that way. An area smaller than the play
  area is not centered: it sits where the door placed it, and the camera holds still there until the
  next transition.
- **The world draws only in the play area.** The HUD strip, and any border or panel the screen is given,
  is reserved: whatever an area reaches past the play area's edge, nothing of the world is drawn there.
- **Player:** eight-direction walking — each held axis moves the player a pixel a tick, so diagonals
  cover both at once; collision resolved per axis, so a diagonal into a wall keeps moving along it; four
  facings, which follow the directions held whether or not the player moves, so the player pushing into
  a wall or a sign turns to face it: a diagonal keeps a facing that is one of its two directions and
  otherwise faces the one pressed more recently, the horizontal one when both were pressed together; a
  two-frame walk animation per facing, eight ticks a frame, resting on its first frame. The
  collision box is the bottom half of the 16×16 frame, 3 px in from each side. All of it is the
  TopDownMovement module, which the player is configured into.
- **Text:** a font is a glyph sheet and the string of characters its cells are; text is sprites, one
  per glyph, wrapped to a width, aligned, shadowed, and typed out a character every few ticks. A font of
  one case draws the other case with it, so text is written in the case it reads best. The HUD strip
  shows the current area's name, right-aligned, typed out again on entering each area.
- **Windows and messages:** a window is a box of any size drawn from a sheet of border pieces of any
  size, with a game's sprites inside it, on one layer; a piece with less room than its own size is cut
  where its room ends. A message is wrapped and cut into pages that fit its box, typed out, and turned
  with one button, an arrow blinking while another page follows. Signs and rocks are read with Item from
  beside them, the player turning to face one by pushing toward it; the world holds still while a message
  is up, and each message is drawn in the border of what is read — wood for a sign, stone for a rock.
- **Actors:** stable ids minted at spawn, per-area spawn tables, one wandering enemy kind.
- **Combat:** a sword swing with a hitbox ahead of the player, enemy knockback and death, player damage
  with knockback and invulnerability (shown by flicker), death and restart.
- **HUD:** hearts in half-heart steps; a heart pickup dropped by enemies.
- **Sound:** one cue per game event, played from the simulation's event stream.
- **Game states:** `Playing`, `Transition`, `GameOver`.

Terms used consistently: **area** (one map with its own art and openings; a room, a screen, a cave),
**layout** (a set of areas sharing a cell size, a view and transition settings; the overworld, a
dungeon), **opening** (a way out of an area, in a wall or at a spot, leading to an opening in another),
**seam** (a seamless join: two openings whose areas meet as one map), **seam depth** (how many seams
away an area in view is drawn), **layer** (of an area: one of its tile maps, drawn at its own depth),
**actor slot** (a named depth in an area where the things moving in it are drawn), **play area** (the
screen below the HUD strip), **camera** (the position in the current area shown at the play area's top-left),
**actor** (anything spawned in an area), **cell** (one 8×8 tile position), **font** (a glyph sheet and
the character of each of its cells), **glyph** (one character's cell, drawn as one sprite), **reveal**
(how much of a passage has been typed out), **border** (a sheet of the pieces a box is drawn from, and
its palette), **window** (a box and what is put inside it, on one layer), **message** (a passage read in
a box, a page at a time), **sign** (a thing in the world that is read: a sign, a rock).

## Hard constraints

- **Everything on screen is a component**: a stateless function, named for the thing it is, that takes
  the state it shows as parameters and returns the engine primitives it is, re-derived from state every
  frame; the frame is the list of what the components return. Depth is which layer a thing is on, and
  a thing not shown is simply not returned — so the game holds no CPU-side screen or tile map, no object
  buffer, no per-cell visibility or priority flag, no hide call, and no save-and-restore of screen
  contents.
- **The simulation has no draw types.** `src/game/` never builds or reads a sprite, tile cell, layer or
  frame, and includes nothing from the renderer or from `src/render/`; it uses the engine's geometry,
  shapes and anchors. Everything that changes over time advances in the tick; rendering only reads.
- **The simulation names what happened** as events; presentation and audio consume them.
- **Every system can be lifted out** into a standalone module: nothing under `modules/` includes a
  project header, and a system meant to be general holds no game constants.
- **Every size a module uses is the art's or the caller's**: a piece, a glyph, a box, a cell is the size
  its sheet or its caller says. The screen size and the 8-pixel cell are this game's choice, held in
  `src/` and `assets/`.
- **The engine is consumed, not modified.** `engine/` is a pinned submodule; what the engine should do
  differently is raised with the engine project.
- **Original content only.** The game is in a genre and uses its mechanics; it recreates no existing
  game's names, characters, art, music, maps or dungeons.
- **Code-facing documentation** — module READMEs, guide pages, every source and build-file comment — is
  written in the present tense, says what the code does, and carries no development history.

## What must be preserved

- The component model of rendering above.
- The layer aggregates are this project's own, in `modules/LayerKit/`, until the engine ships its own form.
  A layer built through them with no options draws exactly as a plain engine layer would: every layer
  field beyond its key, depth, size and content passes through with the engine's own default, and the
  layer's size is the active viewport.
- Walking is the TopDownMovement module's, in `modules/TopDownMovement/`: the game says what a walker is
  and what is solid, and the module moves, turns, animates and draws it.
- The world is WorldKit's, in `modules/WorldKit/`, and the camera CameraKit's, in `modules/CameraKit/`.
  WorldKit composes LayerKit and CameraKit; no module depends on a movement module — movement plugs
  into the world through the engine's types (a solid query, a box, a move), so another kind of movement
  fits the same world.
- Text is TextKit's, in `modules/TextKit/`, on the engine alone. Windows and messages are WindowKit's, in
  `modules/WindowKit/`, on the engine, LayerKit and TextKit; menus will be windows composed into menus,
  in MenuKit. How a window appears and goes is not part of WindowKit yet: a window is shown or it is not.
- Library first: when a system could be general, it is written general and placed where it can be
  lifted out.
- The layout-area-and-opening world model, the three transitions, the camera rules and the reserved
  screen above.
- An area's look as layers and actor slots on one depth axis, with every depth the game's.
- The Game Boy Color idiom at a 60 Hz tick.
